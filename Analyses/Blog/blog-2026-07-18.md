# The AI stack is re-stratifying

The most important AI story right now may not be the next benchmark jump. It may be where the money, trust, and liability move when base intelligence gets cheaper.

A plausible new stack is taking shape. At the bottom, open-weight models are absorbing more production work as capability gaps narrow and inference costs fall. Above that, agent systems need rules for coordination, cost control, search behavior, and auditability. At the human interface, especially in service work, AI is becoming a labor issue as much as a product feature.

That does not prove a clean historical break. The evidence is uneven: some of it comes from reports and benchmarks, some from preprints, and the labor side rests heavily on one visible healthcare dispute. But the pieces point in the same direction. Competitive advantage is moving away from raw model access and toward governance: who controls the workflow, who can inspect it, who pays when it fails, and who gets to say no.

## Base intelligence is getting cheaper

The commoditization claim is easy to overstate, so it needs to be stated narrowly. Open-weight models are not uniformly better than proprietary frontier systems. They do not erase the advantages of large closed labs in every domain. But for a growing share of ordinary production workloads, they appear to be good enough, cheap enough, and controllable enough to change the economics.

The State of Open Source AI report makes the strongest version of this case. It finds that a majority of production tokens now route through open-weight models. It also reports that GPT-4-class inference costs fell from $20 to $0.40 per 1 million tokens over 36 months, and that open models have reached parity with closed models on coding and instruction-following tasks.

Those numbers do not mean base models are interchangeable in all settings. They do mean that model choice is becoming less like choosing a scarce magical engine and more like choosing infrastructure: price, latency, deployment control, licensing, privacy, and operational fit start to matter as much as small differences in benchmark scores.

The common "Linux moment" analogy is useful only if treated as an analogy. Linux did not make operating systems worthless. It changed where value accumulated: support, tooling, cloud infrastructure, integration, security, and enterprise control. Something similar may be happening with AI. If a large class of model capability becomes abundant, then the scarce layer is no longer just the model. It is the system that decides how models are used.

That is the narrow thesis: not that frontier models stop mattering, but that base intelligence is becoming a less durable moat for many applications. The more this holds, the more value moves upward into orchestration, evaluation, and governance.

## Agentic systems need constitutions

If cheap models are the substrate, the next question is coordination. A single model call can be judged by answer quality. A persistent agent system is harder. It searches, delegates, retries, spends money, calls tools, accumulates context, and sometimes gets stuck. You cannot evaluate that kind of system only by asking whether the final answer looked good.

"Agentic constitutions" is a grand phrase for a practical requirement: explicit rules that govern how agents decompose tasks, exchange information, spend budget, recover from failure, and leave an audit trail. This is not yet a mature product category. It is a design space becoming visible.

Several recent papers point at the problem from different angles.

Bridge Evidence argues that static document utility scores do not predict a document's causal value in multi-step agentic search. In simpler terms, a document may matter not because it contains the final answer, but because it helps the agent discover the next useful step. That weakens a common evaluation shortcut: scoring retrieval components in isolation and assuming the score predicts system behavior.

Beyond Success Rate makes a related point for security agents. In real operations, every reasoning step, tool call, and telemetry query has a cost. A security agent that solves a task after burning an unrealistic budget is not equivalent to one that solves it cheaply. Success per dollar is not a cosmetic metric; it changes which systems are actually deployable.

SearchOS-V1 tackles a failure mode that anyone who has watched agents search the web will recognize: repetitive loops when information is missing or search fails. Its contribution is not proof that multi-agent search is solved. It is evidence that robustness, stopping rules, and failure recovery are becoming first-class research problems.

AutoSynthesis pushes the same issue into scientific work. An agentic system for automated meta-analysis has to formulate a search strategy, screen studies, extract data, and synthesize results. Whether or not that system is ready for high-stakes use, the workflow makes the audit problem unavoidable. A reviewer needs to know which studies were excluded, why, and how errors propagate through the pipeline.

Partition, Prompt, Aggregate adds a more mathematical test: if in-context learning behaves like conditional inference, model outputs should obey the law of total probability across partitions. That is a narrow reliability check, not a complete theory of model behavior. But it fits the same direction of travel: evaluation is moving from peak capability toward consistency, cost, and causal usefulness.

None of these papers proves that "agentic constitutions" will become the dominant middleware layer. They are early research artifacts. The useful takeaway is more modest: agent systems need enforceable operating rules. Those rules may live in open-source frameworks, proprietary platforms, enterprise policy engines, or regulation. The exact form is still open.

The moat, if one forms, will not be "we have agents." It will be "we can show what our agents did, why they did it, what it cost, where they failed, and who had authority to override them."

## Human delivery becomes the contested layer

The third layer is less technical and more uncomfortable. When AI makes parts of a service cheaper, the remaining human work does not automatically become more humane. It can become more monitored, more scripted, and more legally contested.

The Kaiser Permanente nurses' dispute is one example worth examining, not proof of a universal trend. Nurses reported that AI systems tracked call length, predicted "unproductivity," and rated empathy or tone during patient calls. They argued that these systems pressured them to rush care, including sensitive interactions. The California Nurses Association made AI surveillance and workplace control part of a major bargaining fight.

The important point is not that this one case predicts every sector. It is that the conflict exposes a mechanism.

First, management introduces AI to measure or optimize service work. Second, the system turns hard-to-measure human judgment, such as empathy, patience, escalation, or risk assessment, into operational metrics. Third, workers argue that the metrics distort the work itself. Fourth, the dispute moves into contracts, legislation, audit rights, and liability.

That mechanism could produce a bifurcated service economy, especially in healthcare, education, finance, and customer support. One tier is low-cost and algorithmically managed: AI triage, scripted human execution, tight productivity metrics, limited discretion. The other tier is explicitly human: more expensive, more protected, and marketed around judgment, empathy, or professional accountability.

This is a prediction, not a fact. It may not happen cleanly. Regulation could require meaningful human oversight across the board. Buyers may reject low-trust automation in sensitive services. AI systems may become good enough at some forms of interaction that the premium tier shrinks. But the Kaiser case shows that labor contracts can become AI governance in practice. They can specify what systems may measure, when workers can contest algorithmic decisions, and what audit rights patients or employees have.

That matters for the stack because the final product is not just a model output. It is a service delivered through institutions. If law, unions, procurement rules, and liability constraints determine how AI reaches people, then the human interface becomes a moat. Not because humans are magically outside the system, but because trust has to be negotiated there.

RoboTTT suggests that a similar issue may eventually reach robotics. The paper reports 8,000-step visuomotor context for robot policies, described as three orders of magnitude beyond prior work, without increasing inference latency. It also reports one-shot imitation from human video and on-the-fly policy improvement. If embodied AI follows the same path as language models, then base robotic capability may also become less scarce over time. But physical agents make governance harder, not easier. A bad search loop wastes tokens. A bad robot policy can break objects or injure people.

That is why the same constitutional layer may extend into the physical world: rules for coordination, audit, stopping, liability, and human override.

## Counterargument

The strongest objection is that this whole structure may be too neat.

Open-weight production share does not prove open models will dominate high-value work. Proprietary labs may keep a durable lead in frontier reasoning, multimodal systems, long-horizon planning, safety tooling, and enterprise integration. If the most profitable applications depend on those capabilities, base intelligence may remain a moat at the top even while cheaper models handle the bulk of tokens.

The agentic layer may also fail to consolidate. "Constitutions" could remain a loose bundle of evals, logs, prompts, policy files, and vendor-specific orchestration tools rather than becoming a coherent layer of the stack. Enterprises often tolerate messy middleware if it works well enough.

The labor argument is even less settled. The Kaiser case may be a sector-specific fight over bad implementation rather than a preview of a broad service split. Better-designed systems could reduce surveillance pressure instead of increasing it. Regulation could flatten the two-tier dynamic by requiring baseline human rights and auditability for everyone, not just premium customers.

Those objections are strong. The thesis should not be read as destiny. It is a framework for what to watch.

If open-weight models keep capturing production volume while costs continue falling, the commodity-base layer gets stronger. If papers like Bridge Evidence, Beyond Success Rate, SearchOS-V1, AutoSynthesis, and Partition, Prompt, Aggregate turn into widely used evaluation and orchestration practices, the constitutional middleware layer becomes more real. If labor disputes over AI surveillance spread from healthcare into other service sectors, the human delivery layer becomes a central governance battleground.

The shift, if it happens, will not be announced by one model release. It will show up in budgets, contracts, audit logs, procurement rules, and worker negotiations. That is less glamorous than a leaderboard. It is probably more important.
